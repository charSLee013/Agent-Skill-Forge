# Editorial Direction

## Outcome Contract

- **Outcome**: the course reads as one guided argument in the learner's language, not a stack of generated sections.
- **Done when**: every page has a distinct editorial job, every paragraph advances that job, and sentence-level polish preserves the subject's meaning and voice.
- **Evidence**: the Course Contract, source terminology, representative lesson, paragraph pass, and read-aloud pass.

## Core Stance

Use these rules as a catalog of editorial smells, not a checklist to maximize. Strong editing preserves what already works and makes the few changes that repair comprehension.

- Meaning and causal order come before elegance.
- The learner's needs and the subject's voice determine register.
- Paragraphs are units of reasoning, not containers for similarly themed sentences.
- Repetition earns its place only when it supports retrieval or deliberate emphasis.
- Concrete objects, actions, evidence, and consequences carry more teaching value than elevated vocabulary.

## Editorial Contract

Set these before drafting:

- learner vocabulary and assumed fluency;
- explanatory stance: tutorial, field guide, reconstruction, critique, or reference;
- term policy for definitions, aliases, symbols, and abbreviations;
- paragraph rhythm for the reading duration and information density;
- evidence voice for sourced facts, derivations, observations, explanations, and transfer;
- one sentence describing how the course should sound when read aloud.

## Lesson Arc

Shape each lesson around its responsibility. A useful arc often contains:

1. a concrete question, task, failure, or observation that creates the need;
2. the causal spine that explains what changes and why;
3. a reconstruction, worked case, trace, or comparison;
4. a boundary, diagnosis, or transfer decision;
5. a handoff to the next capability.

These are responsibilities, not mandatory headings. Merge, reorder, or omit them when the subject calls for a different rhythm.

## Paragraph Pass

Read paragraphs in sequence and label each by its job. Then inspect:

- whether the opening follows from the previous paragraph's close;
- whether every sentence advances the paragraph's claim;
- whether examples arrive after the learner has the concepts needed to read them;
- whether the paragraph closes its idea or deliberately opens the next one;
- whether sentence length and structure vary enough to keep the reasoning audible.

Repair the smallest broken connection first: one reordered clause, one concrete noun, one transition, or one bridging sentence. Split only when a paragraph is doing two reasoning jobs.

## Sentence Pass

- Keep subject, action, and consequence visible.
- Introduce technical terms where they become useful, then use them consistently.
- Tie pronouns and references to unmistakable antecedents.
- Replace vague intensifiers with mechanism, evidence, scale, or conditions.
- Keep qualifications beside the claim they constrain.
- Use lists for real peer sets or sequences, not to avoid composing an argument.
- Read dense explanations aloud and repair cadence without flattening technical precision.

## Smell Catalog

| Smell | Evidence | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Summary echo | The opening, callout, and close restate one sentence | Keep the strongest instance and use the freed space for mechanism or application. |
| Repeated contrast | Paragraphs repeatedly announce what something is not | State the operative object, condition, or consequence directly. Preserve logical negation. |
| Generic transition | A bridge such as "Now let's explore" names no relationship | Name the dependency, consequence, exception, or question that connects the sections. |
| Heading fragments | Short sections interrupt a continuous explanation | Merge sections that share one reasoning job. |
| Abstract noun chain | Actions disappear behind nominalizations | Restore the actor, operation, evidence, and result. |
| Inflated claim | Importance is asserted without mechanism or evidence | Supply the reason and scope, or reduce the claim. |
| False intimacy | The prose invents shared feelings or experience | Address the learner's observable task or difficulty. |
| Uniform cadence | Every sentence has the same length and shape | Vary setup, explanation, evidence, and conclusion according to their jobs. |
| Template voice | Unrelated lessons use identical openings and closings | Let page responsibility and subject matter determine composition. |

## Editorial Pass Order

1. Verify facts, terminology, and causal order.
2. Verify page and section responsibilities.
3. Repair paragraph coherence and progression.
4. Tighten sentences and remove empty repetition.
5. Read representative dense passages aloud.
6. Compare the final prose with the learner, voice, and density fields in the Course Contract.

Stop when further edits would change voice without improving comprehension.

## Gotchas

| What happened | Rule |
|---|---|
| Every flagged phrase was mechanically replaced | Match the smell in context, not the string. |
| Polishing removed domain texture | Restore the source vocabulary, concrete objects, and real constraints. |
| More headings made the page easier to scan but harder to learn | Preserve the reasoning arc; scanning aids support it. |
| A concise edit removed a prerequisite or boundary | The Course Contract's content objects set the minimum depth. |
| A long explanation stayed long because every sentence was correct | Test whether each sentence advances the same paragraph job. |

## Completion

Complete the editorial direction when the representative lesson has a distinct arc, paragraph handoffs are explicit, technical claims retain their scope, repeated template language is absent, and the prose sounds natural at the learner's level.
